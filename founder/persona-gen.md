# LSW buyer personas

These personas are hypotheses until real buyers confirm them. Names, details and quotes are illustrative, not real people or testimonials.

## Assumptions

- Assumption: the buyer age band is 18 to 28 (product-brief.md assumption; facts.md says only "younger customers").
- Assumption: DROP 001 hoodie price lands in the $150 to $225 band shown in competitor-matrix.md. No LSW price is set.
- Assumption: the founder sells only online, with no stockists.
- Assumption: incomes, weekly spend and hours below are estimates for sizing, not data.

One outside fact: 39% of Gen Z clothing buyers found a new apparel brand or product on social media in the prior six months ([eMarketer](https://www.emarketer.com/content/gen-zs-path-purchase)). Year of survey not confirmed; the same source says social is one piece of the path to purchase.

## Persona 1: Janelle Okafor, the faith-first style buyer

**Identity.** 25, Houston. Youth ministry assistant and part-time stylist. Income: Estimate $32,000 to $42,000.

**Day in the life.** Janelle runs a youth group on Wednesdays and posts outfits on Instagram and TikTok between shifts. She saves drop announcements, then loses them in her feed. She wears oversized black pieces and dislikes how most Christian apparel is soft cotton with a verse across the chest. She asks friends for sizing help before she buys anything online.

**Goals.** Primary: build a wardrobe that looks like her taste and quietly says what she believes. Secondary: find pieces she can wear to church and to a concert. Motivation: not feeling split between two identities.

**Pain points.**
1. "Every Christian brand I find looks like a youth camp shirt."
2. "I don't want to spend $180 and find out the fabric is thin."
3. "I can't tell if a small brand will ever ship my order."

**Workarounds.**
- Mainstream streetwear plus a small cross necklace: the faith message is invisible. Estimate: $60 a month on clothes, 3 hours a week browsing.
- Faith-brand tees: wrong fit and style. Estimate: $25 each, worn twice.

**Buying behavior.**
- Discovery: Instagram and TikTok, friends' posts, church and youth-group networks.
- Comparing: reads comments, checks fabric weight, asks for fit photos on a real body.
- Price: will stretch for one hero piece. Assumption: $150 to $200 feels fair if the quality is visible.
- Trust and fit anxiety: high. Wants a size chart with measurements and a clear return policy.
- Trigger to pay: a friend wearing it, or a numbered limited run that will sell out.

**Fit score.** Urgency 4. Willingness to pay 3. Reachability 4. Priority: Primary.

## Persona 2: Marcus Reed, the drop chaser

**Identity.** 21, Atlanta. College student with a retail job. Income: Estimate $14,000 to $22,000.

**Day in the life.** Marcus checks sneaker and streetwear accounts before class and sets alerts for drops at set times. He knows Sp5der and Godspeed prices and watches resale sites for what sold out. He is faith-open and respects a brand with a point of view, but he buys the look first. He has missed drops because checkout was slow.

**Goals.** Primary: own two or three pieces most people at school have not seen. Secondary: build enough closet value to resell if needed. Motivation: status among friends who follow the same accounts.

**Pain points.**
1. "If it sells out in a minute and the site crashes, I'm done with that brand."
2. "I won't pay $200 for a name nobody knows."
3. "Half the small brands look like blank hoodies with a print."

**Workarounds.**
- Resale sites for sold-out drops: markups and fake risk. Estimate: pays 30% over retail, about $60 extra per piece.
- Cheaper lookalikes: wrong fabric. Estimate: $40 hoodie, worn a few times.

**Buying behavior.**
- Discovery: TikTok, Instagram, Discord, friends' drop alerts.
- Comparing: against Sp5der-type pieces; checks resale price as proof of demand.
- Price: compares per piece to resale value. Assumption: stops at about $150 for an unknown brand.
- Trust and fit anxiety: medium. Cares about authenticity and numbered pieces.
- Trigger to pay: visible scarcity and early-access link.

**Fit score.** Urgency 3. Willingness to pay 2. Reachability 4. Priority: Secondary.

## Persona 3: Diego Alvarez, the look-only buyer

**Identity.** 27, Phoenix. Junior graphic designer. Income: Estimate $48,000 to $60,000.

**Day in the life.** Diego follows designers and music artists, and notices stitching and labels. He buys few pieces a year but pays for detail. He is not religious and will skip anything that feels preachy. A cross or star motif is fine to him if it looks original.

**Goals.** Primary: find well-made, original dark pieces that are not hype logos. Secondary: support small designers. Motivation: taste and craft.

**Pain points.**
1. "If the message is loud, I assume it's not for me."
2. "Oversized should mean heavy fabric, not a big thin shirt."
3. "Sizing on small brands is a gamble and returns are a hassle."

**Workarounds.**
- Vintage and thrift finds: inconsistent sizing. Estimate: 4 hours a week looking, $120 a month.
- Gallery Dept-type pieces when saving up: price far above his range ($735 and up for hoodies per [END. Clothing](https://www.endclothing.com/us/brands/gallery-dept)).

**Buying behavior.**
- Discovery: Instagram, designer forums, creators he follows.
- Comparing: zooms into product photos, checks fabric weight and construction.
- Price: pays $200 or more if quality is evident. Assumption.
- Trust and fit anxiety: medium. Needs honest photos and measurements.
- Trigger to pay: craft details shown close up.

**Fit score.** Urgency 2. Willingness to pay 4. Reachability 3. Priority: Tertiary.

## Persona priority matrix

| Persona | Urgency | Willingness to pay | Reachability | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Janelle, faith-first | 4 | 3 | 4 | 11 |
| Marcus, drop chaser | 3 | 2 | 4 | 9 |
| Diego, look-only | 2 | 4 | 3 | 9 |

**Build for Janelle first.** She has the sharpest unmet need: the gap competitor-matrix.md found between faith brands and rhinestone brands. She can be reached through youth groups and church networks, which cost little for a founder with no audience. Marcus is next, because numbered limited runs give him a reason to buy and post. Diego should be able to buy without feeling preached to, so keep the message in the details, not the slogans.

## What needs real people

- Whether Janelle-type buyers exist in numbers and will pay $150 or more for an unknown brand.
- Whether the faith message attracts or repels Marcus and Diego types.
- Fit and fabric worries: which proof (measurements, fabric weight, return policy) removes them.
