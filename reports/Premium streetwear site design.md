# Make LSW sell scarcity, not just style

> **Read this first: how the evidence was gathered.** The research team could not load most source pages. Brand sites (Represent, Fear of God, Corteiz, Aimé Leon Dore, Cole Buxton, Stüssy, Palace, Rhude), Baymard, the FTC, the UK government and legislation sites, Hypebeast and Shopify Help all failed with DNS errors, proxy blocks or HTTP 000. Nearly every finding below comes from **search-result snippets and summaries**, not full-page reads, and no benchmark brand's live site was seen first-hand. Treat every specific figure (percentages, penalty amounts, dates, conversion lifts) as **"re-check at the primary source before quoting publicly."** This matters most for the legal material, where the wording came from law-firm summaries rather than the statutes and reports themselves.

LSW's look is already in the right family. A dark monochrome palette, a chrome wordmark, wide uppercase display type and mono labels sit squarely in the "restrained" camp that practitioners link with premium positioning. So the biggest gains won't come from a new aesthetic. They come from three things the site does not yet do well. First, it should **turn DROP 001 into a real event**: a fixed, published release time, a gated early-access window for the signup list, and edition sizes that match the actual Redis inventory. Second, it should **fix the product page basics that apparel shoppers rely on**: size and fit information next to the size selector, five or more consistent images, shipping and returns stated before checkout, and Apple Pay or Google Pay shown early. Third, it should **apply more typographic and motion discipline**: very large display type set against tiny mono labels, near-black rather than pure black, a shared-element image transition, and CSS scroll reveals. The best-evidenced items are the Baymard sizing and image findings, the WCAG 2.2 requirements and the consumer-protection rules. The drop-mechanics patterns are well documented as brand practice but have no conversion data behind them. Most of the visual and motion advice, and every 3D or "+X% conversion" figure, is either practitioner opinion or a vendor claim. LSW's existing pieces (Redis per-SKU stock, the limit of 3, Stripe Checkout, the early-access list) are the core of an honest drop system. That makes LSW unusually well placed to **use scarcity that is true and provable**, which is both the strongest premium signal and the only legally safe kind.

## Top drop brands sell through gating and a fixed clock

The pattern with the best documentation across the benchmark brands is **gating plus a predictable release time**. It is not a particular visual style. Corteiz sells "via a password-protected ecommerce site or gamified physical drops," and shares codes on social media ([BoF](https://www.businessoffashion.com/community/people/clint); [Euronews 2023](https://www.euronews.com/2023/03/17/the-rise-of-corteiz-inside-the-genius-marketing-strategies-of-londons-hottest-streetwear-b)). A 2024-era article questioned whether Corteiz still does this ([Undiscovered Mag](https://www.undiscoveredmag.com/post/is-the-password-method-dead)), so treat it as an established pattern rather than confirmed current practice. Represent reportedly launches on its app first, sometimes a full day ahead. The website stays password-locked during that window, and a push notification carries the password ([Venn Apps case study](https://www.vennapps.com/case-studies/represent), a self-interested vendor source). Its upper loyalty tier gets early access to weekly drops ([eCommerce Fastlane](https://ecommercefastlane.com/lessons-in-fashion-loyalty-with-represent-clothing/)). The large brands all publish a fixed clock. Kith runs a "Monday Program" at **11am EST** ([Kith](https://kith.com/collections/kith-monday-program)). Palace released Spring 2026 Drop 11 at **11:00 BST** ([Hypebeast DE](https://hypebeast.com/de/2026/4/palace-skateboards-spring-2026-drop-11-release-info)). Sp5der ran daily 12:00 PM EST releases during its May 2026 "Worldwide Five Day" ([Hypebeast](https://hypebeast.com/2026/5/sp5der-worldwide-five-day-nyc-popup)). The sequential naming ("Drop 11", Supreme's "Week N") means **DROP 001 already follows the genre's convention**.

LSW can copy the homepage archetypes as well. One is editorial-first: a full-bleed campaign hero followed by a collection story and then a product grid. Fear of God's flagship was rebuilt around long-form narrative templates that sit beside drops ([Kettle](https://www.wearekettle.com/case-studies/fear-of-god)). Kettle also claims +150% brand recall and "the best week of sales" in Fear of God's history, but those are **unverified agency claims** from roughly 2023-24. The other archetype is stripped down: Supreme's homepage has "zero call to actions and only one graphic, its logo." The author warns that this only works because of brand equity ([BDOW](https://bdow.com/stories/supreme-marketing/)). For a brand with three SKUs and no photography, the practical version combines the two. The homepage should show a single, mostly typographic drop moment, with a short FAITH / DISCIPLINE / PURPOSE editorial band below it. **Neither layout needs photography.**

Owned channels are the other half of the mechanism. In a Klaviyo case study, HOMAGE subscribers who opted into early-access releases clicked SMS at **64%, against a 10.5% average** ([Klaviyo/HOMAGE](https://www.klaviyo.com/customers/case-studies/homage-apparel)). Sneaker Politics' hottest drops "can sell out in about a minute" ([Klaviyo](https://klaviyo.com/customers/case-studies/sneaker-politics-sms-marketing)). Both are **vendor data**. When Corteiz's Instagram briefly vanished, the founder redirected followers to WhatsApp ([Complex](https://www.complex.com/style/a/tracewilliamcowen/corteiz-instagram-mysteriously-removed)). LSW's early-access signup should therefore become the actual unlock mechanism, using signed, expiring links emailed before a published public time, rather than a vague promise. LSW should also add a second owned channel such as SMS, with explicit opt-in.

## Product pages fail on sizing far more often than on style

The best-evidenced conversion findings concern apparel product pages, and they point to information, not decoration. In Baymard's benchmark, **83% of desktop and 87% of mobile apparel sites fail to provide sufficient sizing information** ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/research-articles/apparel-size-information)). In Baymard's testing, **84% of participants used sizing info** to choose a size, and some guessed or abandoned the purchase when they could not find it ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/apparel-size-information)). Baymard recommends putting the size-guide link directly beside the size selector, using buttons rather than a dropdown, and giving measurements in both inches and centimetres ([Baymard size guide examples](https://baymard.com/ecommerce-design-examples/size-guide); [Baymard apparel](https://baymard.com/research-articles/apparel-5-best-practices)). NN/G found that luxury shoppers were "confused and annoyed" when details such as measurements were missing ([NN/G](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/luxury-principles-ecommerce-design/)). For LSW this is the cheapest premium upgrade on the list. A precise spec block costs nothing and fits the DISCIPLINE pillar. It should cover GSM weight, composition, a fit note ("boxy, drop shoulder"), a measurement table, and the edition size.

Images come next. On a product page, **56% of users' first action was to explore the images**, and **42% tried to judge scale from them** ([Baymard, older study](https://baymard.com/research-articles/in-scale-product-images)). Baymard advises at least 3-5 images and 5-15 for apparel, and it says worn items need human-model context ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/ecommerce-ux-best-practices)). This is LSW's one real gap that renders cannot fully close. The workable approach is five or more renders per SKU (front, back, three-quarter, two or three detail crops, and a graphic measurement card), all with identical lighting, angle and background. Fit should be shown with a CLO3D or Blender avatar render of the boxy silhouette until real sample photography exists. A practitioner review warns that generic mockup templates "can look generic" and skew toward slim fits ([Studio Innate](https://shop.studioinnate.com/top-5-streetwear-mockup-tools-for-2026-ranked-reviewed/)). In practice, **consistency across the three SKUs matters more than how many images there are**.

Sticky add-to-cart buttons are popular but have weak evidence behind them. Agency A/B write-ups disagree on direction. One reports **+8.7% add-to-cart but lower revenue**, and another reports +15.1% conversion ([cleancommit](https://cleancommit.io/ab-tests/mobile-bottom-sticky-add-to-cart-button/); [cleancommit](https://cleancommit.io/ab-tests/sticky-mobile-add-to-cart-button/)). Baymard's guidance is that a sticky button should be visually distinct, surrounded by white space and **not full-width** ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/ecommerce-ux-best-practices)). LSW should ship one on mobile, follow those rules, and watch revenue per session rather than add-to-cart rate.

Checkout evidence lines up with LSW's stack. The average documented cart abandonment rate is **70.22%** ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate)). Unexpected extra costs are the top fixable reason, cited by somewhere between 39% and 48% of respondents depending on the dataset; the secondary figures conflict ([Red Stag](https://redstagfulfillment.com/percentage-of-online-shoppers-abandon-their-cart/)). Stripe reports a **~10% conversion uplift from offering Apple Pay** in an A/B test ([Stripe Sessions 2024](https://stripe.com/sessions/2024/a-look-under-stripes-hood-using-data-to-demystify-payments)). It also reports **~2x conversion when Apple Pay appears early** through the Express Checkout Element rather than only at the end ([Stripe blog](https://stripe.com/blog/testing-the-conversion-impact-of-50-plus-global-payment-methods)). These are vendor figures without published methodology, but their direction is consistent. LSW should state shipping cost and delivery time on the product page and in the cart drawer, enable wallets in Stripe Checkout, and consider adding Express Checkout in the drawer.

## Restraint, scale contrast and near-black read as premium

The visual guidance is consistent but rests mostly on practitioner opinion. Cheap sites "fill space with bold claims, bright colors, urgent messaging," while premium sites use a neutral base, one or two accents and wider margins ([muffingroup](https://muffingroup.com/blog/luxury-websites/); [Bliss Creative](https://www.blisscreativestudio.com/post/what-gives-a-website-that-luxury-look)). "Tight, compressed type suggests efficiency and value," while open type reads as craft ([Lettertype Studio](https://lettertypestudio.com/what-makes-a-font-feel-luxury/)). NN/G's whitespace research does support easier scanning ([NN/G](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/whitespace/)). Several statistics circulate widely but **could not be traced to a primary source and should not be quoted**: "luxury brands use 2-3x more whitespace," "10-20% higher conversion (IxDF)" and "up to 300% perceived value."

For LSW, the main risk is wide uppercase type used everywhere. The upgrade is **extreme scale contrast**. The wide display face should be reserved for one huge hero line and section titles, set in a fluid `clamp()` with tight leading. Body text and product names should go into a quieter grotesque, and the mono face should be kept for 11-12px tracked labels. A variable font with a `wdth` axis, loaded through `next/font`, lets a single file cover both display and UI widths ([Google Fonts width axis](https://fonts.google.com/knowledge/glossary/width_axis)). The tone also needs adjusting. Pure black with bright text causes halation, and #121212-style dark greys with off-white text are the usual reference ([Uxcel](https://uxcel.com/blog/12-principles-of-dark-mode-design-627); [UX Planet](https://uxplanet.org/dark-mode-ui-design-best-practices-8d3a00a83924)). The one sourced benchmark palette, Aimé Leon Dore's, uses ink #181818 according to an unofficial summary ([shadcn.io](https://www.shadcn.io/design/aime-leon-dore)). A 3-4 step grey ladder with hairline borders, plus a static, very low-opacity grain tile, adds print-like depth. Animated grain should be avoided because of its CPU cost ([CSS-Tricks](https://css-tricks.com/?p=351302)). The chrome wordmark should remain the single "material" moment.

Motion is worth doing when it is native and restrained. Next.js supports React's `<ViewTransition>` behind `experimental.viewTransition`. That allows a shared-element morph from a product card to the product-page hero, but the docs flag it as **experimental and not recommended for production** ([Next.js docs](https://nextjs.org/docs/app/api-reference/config/next-config-js/viewTransition)). It should be wrapped so the site works the same without it. CSS scroll-driven animations shipped in Safari 26 and have been in Chromium since version 115 ([WebKit](https://webkit.org/blog/17101/a-guide-to-scroll-driven-animations-with-just-css/)), so they can replace JavaScript reveals behind `@supports`. Any marquee that runs longer than 5 seconds needs a pause control under WCAG 2.2.2 ([Rocket Validator](https://rocketvalidator.com/accessibility-validation/marquee)). Custom cursors carry accessibility risk for little payoff ([Eric Bailey](https://ericwbailey.design/writing/dont-use-custom-css-mouse-cursors)). A short looping render video makes a cheap "campaign film" hero if it is muted, uses autoplay and `playsinline`, and has a preloaded poster ([web.dev](https://web.dev/learn/performance/video-performance?hl=fr); [DebugBear](https://debugbear.com/blog/optimize-video-lcp)). All 3D-viewer conversion claims ("94% higher conversion," "35%+ engagement") are **vendor figures that have not been verified** ([Fibbl](https://fibbl.com/integration/shopify-3d-product-viewer/); [Sayduck](https://www.sayduck.com/post/best-3d-product-viewer-apps-for-shopify-2026-comparison)). Full-page WebGL is not worth the mobile cost. In Next.js 16, `priority` on `next/image` is deprecated in favour of `preload`, `loading="eager"` or `fetchPriority="high"` for the LCP image ([Next.js Image docs](https://nextjs.org/docs/pages/api-reference/components/image)).

Accessibility constraints are hard requirements, not trends. WCAG 2.2 AA requires that a focused element is **not entirely hidden by sticky bars or slide-outs** (2.4.11) and that tap targets are **at least 24×24 CSS px** (2.5.8), with 44×44 as the better target ([W3C](https://w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/new-in-22/)). On a dark theme, grey secondary text and thin borders are the most likely contrast failures. Size buttons and swatches are the most likely target-size failures.

## Scarcity must be true, provable and never padded

LSW's scarcity signals are valuable precisely because they can be true. The law in the US, UK and EU converges on **truthfulness and verifiability**. The FTC staff report "Bringing Dark Patterns to Light" (Sept 2022) names several deceptive patterns: false scarcity claims, countdown timers that imply a non-existent deadline or reset at zero, "nearly sold out" claims when stock is ample, and false "others are viewing or buying" claims. Liability turns on the **net impression** of the design ([Venable](https://www.venable.com/insights/blogs/2022/09/the-ftc-brings-more-light-to-dark-patterns-in-new); [Hunton](https://www.huntonprivacyblog.com/2022/09/27/ftc-releases-report-on-dark-patterns/)). The report is guidance, not a binding rule.

The UK CMA's 2023 open letter addresses scarcity, popularity and countdown claims ([RPC](https://www.rpclegal.com/snapshots/advertising-and-marketing/summer-2023/cma-open-letter-to-businesses-on-urgency-and-price-reduction-claims/)). Under it, a bare, true "only five left" is not unlawful in itself. "Selling fast – only 3 left" is likely misleading if there is no real shortage **or if new stock is arriving shortly** ([Hogan Lovells](https://hlc.com/en/publications/online-claims-that-put-unfair-pressure-on-consumers-new-uk-guidance-on-what-to-avoid)). A countdown is a problem if the offer continues past it, recurs soon after, or resets on refresh ([Gowling WLG](https://gowlingwlg.com/en/insights-resources/articles/2024/cma-investigates-unfair-online-sales-practices)). Enforcement is now real. On **22 May 2026** the High Court approved a consent order against Emma Sleep that stopped misleading countdown timers and "high demand" banners ([Mondaq/Lewis Silkin](https://www.mondaq.com/uk/dodd-frank-consumer-protection-act/1796032/uk-consumer-law-revolution-emma-sleep-case-is-partly-settled-but-the-other-part-is-keeping-us-awake-at-night)). The DMCC Act 2024 gave the CMA direct enforcement powers ([Ashurst](https://www.ashurst.com/en/insights/mind-the-nudge-the-dmccs-crackdown-on-manipulative-online-design/)). The EU UCPD Annex I blacklist also applies ([legislation.gov.uk copy](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2005/29/annexes/data.htm)). The researcher did not verify the specific Annex I item on false "very limited time" claims, so check it on EUR-Lex.

Reviews carry their own penalty regime. The FTC's final rule banning fake reviews and testimonials took effect **21 October 2024**. It bans fake or AI-generated reviews, undisclosed insider reviews, review suppression, conditioning incentives on positive sentiment, and fake social-proof indicators such as bought followers ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials)). Penalties run **up to $51,744 per violation**, and each fake review can count separately ([Manatt](https://manatt.com/insights/newsletters/client-alert/ftc-issues-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-and-tes)). The research did not check whether the rule has been challenged since 2024. The commercial case for honesty points the same way. Older Spiegel research found that **five reviews raised purchase likelihood by ~270%** compared with none, and that purchase probability peaks at a **4.2-4.5 average**, not 5.0 ([Spiegel Research Center](https://www.xait.com/hubfs/Spiegel_Online-Review_eBook_Jun2017_FINAL.pdf)). A wall of perfect reviews is both illegal if faked and less persuasive. LSW should launch with no review widget, collect reviews after delivery, and display all of them.

Renders raise a parallel issue. The question is not whether AI or 3D was used. It is whether the image creates a **misleading impression of fit, fabric, sheen or colour**, and a label does not cure a false depiction ([Revision Legal](https://revisionlegal.com/corporate/revision-legal/ai-generated-product-descriptions-legal-risks-ecommerce/); [Style3D](https://www.style3d.com/blog/?p=17487)). Both are secondary summaries. One source says the EU AI Act requires labels on photo-like AI imagery from 2 August 2026. That claim is **unverified** against the regulation, so legal review is advised. The practical approach turns the disclosure into a design element: a mono caption such as "CONCEPT RENDER — SAMPLE PHOTOGRAPHY TO FOLLOW," no renders of colourways that are not confirmed for production, and the important specifications stated in text.

The table below summarises what these rules mean for LSW. It was derived from the sources above and should be read alongside legal advice.

| Allowed when true | Avoid |
|---|---|
| "Edition of N", where N equals the starting Redis inventory | Restocking a "limited" piece without saying so |
| A countdown to one fixed, server-side DROP 001 time | Per-visitor, evergreen or resetting timers |
| Live stock counts and sold-out states read straight from Redis | "X viewing now" or "sold in the last hour" counters that are not real per-product data |
| "Limit 3 per customer", enforced server-side | Early-access windows that quietly extend after saying "ends soon" |
| Real reviews, all shown, collected after delivery | Seeded, staff or AI-written reviews; bought followers |
| Clearly captioned concept renders | Renders of unconfirmed colourways or an idealised fit |

One decision governs the scarcity copy. **Decide now whether DROP 001 will ever be restocked.** If a restock is possible, avoid "only X left" urgency lines and "never again" claims. Keep Redis snapshots so every edition and stock claim can be substantiated later.

## Prioritised upgrade list

The table ranks each change by expected impact for the effort involved. Evidence strength uses four grades. **Hard** means a usability benchmark, controlled study, standard or law. **Practice** means a pattern documented at named brands with no outcome data. **Vendor** means data from a company selling the solution. **Opinion** means practitioner advice or the researchers' own inference.

| # | Page | Change | Why | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Product pages | Put a size-guide link beside the size **buttons**. Add a measurement table in inches and cm, a fit note ("boxy, drop shoulder") and a spec block (GSM, composition, edition size, care), with details in accordions | 83-87% of apparel sites fail on sizing, and 84% of users rely on it | **Hard** (Baymard, NN/G) |
| 2 | Product pages, cart drawer | Show the shipping cost or free-shipping line, delivery estimate and returns/exchange policy before Stripe. Enable Apple Pay, Google Pay and Link, and consider Express Checkout in the drawer | Extra costs are the top fixable abandonment reason; wallets lift conversion | **Hard** (abandonment) + **Vendor** (Stripe lifts) |
| 3 | Product pages | Use 5+ consistent renders per SKU (front, back, three-quarter, detail crops, measurement card) plus an avatar fit render, all under the same lighting and background. Make them full-width and swipeable on mobile | 56% of users look at images first; 42% judge scale from them | **Hard** (Baymard; on-model gap noted) |
| 4 | DROP 001 page, middleware, API | Publish one fixed UTC release time from a server constant, with a countdown that never resets. Refuse purchases server-side before T-0. Send early-access subscribers signed, expiring links ahead of public opening | Core drop mechanic at Corteiz, Represent, Kith and Palace; fake timers are illegal | **Practice** + **Hard** (law) |
| 5 | Drop and product pages | Show "Edition of N" (equal to the Redis start count), "Limit 3 per customer", live stock and a sold-out state drawn from Redis. Do not show fake viewer counters | Honest, provable scarcity; stating limits up front is recommended | **Hard** (law) + **Practice** |
| 6 | Checkout and reserve routes | Use an atomic Redis Lua reservation with a TTL hold, enforce the cap of 3 per account, email or payment method server-side, add Vercel BotID on `/api/checkout` and the reserve route, rate-limit, and add an optional Redis token queue | Prevents overselling and bot sell-outs, so scarcity stays fair ([Vercel BotID](https://vercel.com/docs/botid/get-started)) | **Practice** (Shopify, CrowdHandler equivalents) |
| 7 | Home | Make the hero a single drop moment: huge wide type for "DROP 001", the edition count and date in mono, and one render cut-out or a 6-10s looping render video with a poster. Below it, a type-only FAITH / DISCIPLINE / PURPOSE band, then a 3-up product grid showing name, colour count and price only | Brands present products through a campaign, not a sales grid; a text hero gives the fastest LCP | **Practice** + **Opinion** |
| 8 | Global design system | Use near-black (#0b0b0c to #181818) with a grey elevation ladder and off-white text. Reserve the wide display face for the hero and section titles, use a quieter grotesque for body text, and keep mono at 11-12px tracked. Use a fluid ~5-step scale, hairline borders and a static grain overlay at ~0.06 | Restraint and open type read as premium; pure black causes halation | **Opinion** (practitioner) |
| 9 | Global | Apply WCAG 2.2 AA: targets of 24px or more (44px preferred) for size buttons, swatches and steppers; focus never hidden by the sticky add-to-cart or drawer; focus trapped in the drawer; contrast checks on greys over grain; a global reduced-motion fallback | A legal and standards baseline; dark themes fail it easily | **Hard** (W3C) |
| 10 | All images | Use AVIF/WebP, accurate `sizes`, 4:5 containers, `preload`/`fetchPriority` on the LCP image from a Server Component, a dark blur placeholder, and fonts via `next/font` | Protects LCP and CLS on an image-led site | **Hard** (Next.js docs) + **Opinion** |
| 11 | Shop grid to product page | Add a shared-element View Transition with a fallback, CSS `animation-timeline: view()` reveals, and a second-image hover swap only under `(hover: hover)`. Do not add custom cursors, scroll-jacking or animated grain | A premium feel with no JS cost; gimmicks hurt accessibility | **Opinion**; the API is experimental |
| 12 | Mobile product page | Add a non-full-width sticky add-to-cart surrounded by white space, and track revenue per session | Published A/B results conflict | **Vendor**, mixed |
| 13 | DROP 001 after sell-out | Keep the page as an archive (renders, edition per SKU, "Sold out"). Change the call to action to "Get notified for DROP 002". Only offer "Notify me when back" if a restock is genuinely possible | Truthful post-drop state that also captures demand | **Practice** + **Hard** (law) |
| 14 | Image captions | Caption renders "CONCEPT RENDER — SAMPLE PHOTOGRAPHY TO FOLLOW", replace them with real sample photos once samples exist, and never render unconfirmed colourways | Avoids a misleading impression; reads as deliberate pre-drop culture | **Hard**-adjacent (FTC standard via secondary sources) |
| 15 | Trust pages, reviews | Add a short founder and "why" page, a real contact email and size-exchange terms. Collect genuine reviews after delivery and show all of them | Trust for an unknown brand comes from complete information; fake reviews carry a $51,744-per-violation risk | **Hard** (FTC rule, academic) + **Opinion** |
| 16 | Garment and site (later) | Add a numbered interior tag ("07/150") with a QR or NFC code linked to a `/verify/<serial>` page | Puts the edition story into the garment, as Balenciaga and IUTD do ([NFCW](https://www.nfcw.com/?p=86905)) | **Practice**; no outcome data |

Items 1 to 6 change what shoppers know and what the system guarantees. Items 7 to 11 change how the site feels. A practical sequence is to ship 1 to 6 before DROP 001 opens and work through the rest as polish.

## Conclusion

LSW does not mainly need to look more like Fear of God. It needs the operational credibility that turns a limited run into a fair, provable event, and it already owns the hardest parts of that system. The evidence is strongest on unglamorous work: sizing data, honest costs, early wallets and accessible controls. It is weakest on exactly the things that make redesigns exciting, such as motion, 3D, whitespace ratios and agency "+150%" claims. The sensible split is to build the hard-evidenced items as requirements, treat the visual system as taste to be judged on the brand's own terms, and test sticky add-to-cart buttons rather than assume they help.

There is also a deeper opportunity. In a genre full of fake timers and padded "only 2 left" badges, a brand built on FAITH, DISCIPLINE and PURPOSE can make verifiability its signature: edition counts that match the inventory, a clock that never resets, and reviews shown unfiltered. That choice is both on-brand and legally safe. Before any figure in this report is quoted publicly or used in marketing, re-check it at the primary source, because the research behind it was assembled from snippets of pages the team could not open.
